# Sources for write-plainly

These sources influenced the skill. The skill combines selected principles from them with my own preferences; it does not reproduce a standard or another skill. Study the named writers' explanatory methods without imitating their personalities or treating their arguments as established facts. The links are as collected when the skill was drafted and have not all been rechecked.

## Style guides and standards

- [ASTM, Form and Style for ASTM Standards](https://www.astm.org/v3/assets/blt5eb0a2cb04534832/blt110aa33ef5eaa872/69e24e362ea600fed3edd4fe/bluebook_FormStyle_march2026.pdf): scope, procedures, terminology, and interpretable quantities; especially A5, A18-A22, E, G5, and G19.
- [NASA, Technical Documentation Style Guide](https://standards.nasa.gov/sites/default/files/standards/KSC/F/0/ksc-df-107_rev_f_07082017.pdf): direct language, consistent terms, voice, and noun sequences; especially sections 3.1-3.8.
- [ASD-STE100, Issue 9](https://www.asd-ste100.org/assets/files/ASD-STE100_ISSUE9.pdf) and [ISO House Style](https://www.iso.org/ISO-house-style.html): selected principles for unambiguous instructions and modality, without the controlled vocabulary or the strict sentence-length limits.
- Google: [paragraphs](https://developers.google.com/tech-writing/one/paragraphs), [clear sentences](https://developers.google.com/tech-writing/one/clear-sentences), and [anthropomorphism](https://developers.google.com/style/anthropomorphism). [NIST author guidance](https://www.nist.gov/nist-research-library/nist-technical-series-publications-author-instructions): plain language that preserves technical meaning.

## Writers whose explanations the skill uses as models

- Karpathy: [A Recipe for Training Neural Networks](https://karpathy.github.io/2019/04/25/recipe/) and [Pong from Pixels](https://karpathy.github.io/2016/05/31/rl/): concrete operations, explanations of why checks work, and one example carried through successive concepts.
- Amodei: [Machines of Loving Grace](https://darioamodei.com/essay/machines-of-loving-grace) and [On DeepSeek and Export Controls](https://darioamodei.com/post/on-deepseek-and-export-controls): explicit assumptions and distinctions that support a quantitative argument.
- METR: [Measuring AI Ability to Complete Long Software Tasks](https://metr.org/blog/2025-03-19-measuring-ai-ability-to-complete-long-tasks/) and [Changing our Developer Productivity Experiment Design](https://metr.org/blog/2026-02-24-uplift-update/): operational definitions, scoped results, and explanations of how a limitation affects an estimate.

These six pieces, plus Google's paragraph guide, also served as the false-positive check for the linters. On roughly 32,000 words of them, sloplint 0.10.0 raised almost nothing except em dashes, which these authors use and this skill bans by preference. slop-guard 0.5.0 was noisier: its repeated-phrase rule flagged ordinary technical repetition ("the log probability of"), so `slop-guard.jsonl` is the packaged default config with that rule removed. Its word list also fired on words that careful writers use literally ("robust," "societal"). A later check on matched human and agent text (`eval/README.md`) found its word, phrase, and tone lists firing more often on the human side, so the config now switches them off, and the skill treats any single word hit as a prompt to check, not an error.

## Research on editing and judging

- Gooding, Lopez-Rivilla, and Grefenstette, [Writing as a testbed for open ended agents](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.19711) (2025): in their comparison of LLM co-writers, models favored adding and elaborating content, while human editors more often deleted, simplified, and restructured. Raters favored deletions over insertions. This is the basis for the skill's preference for removing over adding.
- Shaib, Chakrabarty, Garcia-Olano, and Wallace, [Measuring AI "Slop" in Text](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.19163) (2025): binary "slop" judgments were somewhat subjective, while span-level problems tracked dimensions such as coherence and relevance. This is why the reviewer reports specific spans and gives no overall score.
- Chakrabarty et al., [AI-Slop to AI-Polish?](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.07532) (2025): general LLMs barely beat random on a consolidated writing-quality preference benchmark, while reward models trained on expert edits did better. The skill does not use their model, which was trained mostly on literary writing.
- Pan, He, Bowman, and Feng, [Spontaneous Reward Hacking in Iterative Self-Refinement](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.04549) (2024): when one model both revised essays and judged the revisions in a loop, the judge's ratings rose while human ratings stayed flat or fell. A judge that had not seen the loop's history tracked the human ratings. This is why the review runs once, in fresh subagents that do not share the writer's context.
- Russell, Karpinska, and Iyyer, [People who frequently use ChatGPT for writing tasks are accurate and robust detectors of AI-generated text](https://aclanthology.org/2025.acl-long.267/) (ACL 2025): readers who use LLMs heavily for their own writing identified model-written nonfiction articles almost without error. They pointed to vocabulary, sentence structure, and vague, upbeat conclusions as their cues. Readers who rarely use LLMs scored near chance. This is the evidence that obvious model tics cost reader attention, even though the skill gives sounding human little weight.

## Rule lists and model tells

- [bheijden/slop](https://github.com/bheijden/slop), `research/audit.md` (Apache-2.0): an audit of public slop rule lists on matched pairs of human texts written before 2022 and model texts written from the same brief. Most rules never fired, and the default rule sets fired seven to ten times more often on the human technical documentation and academic prose than on the model versions. The em dash appeared in 44% of the human texts and in none of the model texts, and the human texts used parentheses freely where the model texts used none. This is why the skill treats word-list hits as prompts to check, and why a lint rule that fires more on good human prose than on agent drafts gets demoted. The audit's limits are its size (three pairs per register) and its single generator model.

  The audit's figures come from an earlier version of the corpus with 18 pairs in six registers. The version that `eval/README.md` uses has 24 pairs across twice as many registers. On it, the em-dash rate is nearly equal on the two sides (three of the model texts have one), and the model texts still use no parentheses.
- [Wikipedia, Signs of AI writing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing): a descriptive catalog of recurring patterns, not a reliable test of authorship or a universal style code. Its revision of 2026-11-19 moves em-dash overuse to "Historical indicators," because newer models are tuned to avoid the em dash and the resulting lack of punctuation variety "has become its own sign." [sloptells](https://sloptells.com), a dated catalog of tells measured against matched human writing in social and forum registers, also lists the em dash as a myth, and names uniform sentence cadence as the current rhythm tell. The skill keeps its em-dash ban as a preference, and its rule on sentence length asks for variety that follows the content.
- Wikipedia's [Words to watch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Manual_of_Style/Words_to_watch) and the phrase list in [hardikpandya/stop-slop](https://github.com/hardikpandya/stop-slop/blob/main/references/phrases.md) (MIT license): with "Signs of AI writing," the sources for `lint_prose.py`'s lists of model tells (in `lint_prose_patterns.py`) and for the announcing phrases in its `stock-emphasis` rule. The lists keep only entries that the careful human prose in `eval/README.md`'s corpora rarely uses, and leave out what sloplint already flags.

## Tools and other skills

- [sloplint](https://github.com/benjaminjackson/sloplint) by Benjamin Jackson (MIT license): regex rules for rhetorical model tells, run by `check-prose.sh`. The `prose-reviewer` agent's checks for generic paragraphs and sentences, no news, wrap-up, throat-clearing, self-narration, promotional evaluation, unnamed authority, stock figures, maxims, verdict endings, and devices are reworded from the judge questions in sloplint's `docs/JUDGE.md` and `lib/sloplint/judge/rules.rb`. Two findings from its judge design shaped the reviewers. A judge asked whether a model wrote a sentence answered backwards, rating edited human prose as the most machine-written, so neither reviewer judges authorship. And when the judge could not follow the content, it preferred smooth, generic text to dense specialist prose, so the reviewer is told that density is not a defect.
- [slop-guard](https://github.com/eric-tramel/slop-guard): rule-based scoring for structure, rhythm, and sentence patterns, also run by `check-prose.sh` (its word and phrase lists are switched off in this skill's config; see `eval/README.md`).
- Agent skills: [Docker Write](https://github.com/docker/docs/blob/main/.agents/skills/write/SKILL.md), [Cloudflare Prose](https://github.com/cloudflare/computer/blob/main/.agents/skills/prose/SKILL.md), and [Writing Clearly and Concisely](https://github.com/obra/the-elements-of-style/blob/main/skills/writing-clearly-and-concisely/SKILL.md).
- Editing skills: [General Writing](https://github.com/msimchowitz/writing-skills/blob/main/for-agents/general-writing/SKILL.md), [Humanizer](https://github.com/blader/humanizer/blob/main/SKILL.md), [No AI Slop](https://github.com/petergyang/no-ai-slop/blob/main/skills/no-ai-slop/SKILL.md), and [ASD-STE100 skill](https://github.com/danyuchn/asd-ste100-skill/blob/master/SKILL.md).
